The short version

Benson Mtsotso is the developer behind LegendAlive Studio. He designs, builds and operates the studio's products on his own: the interface, the engine, the data layer, the commercial plumbing and the pipeline that deploys them.

Software began as a hobby and became a practice. Two products are live and a third is in development. It runs alongside a career in operations management, not instead of one.

The work is not organised around a domain. It is organised around problems that have a clear enough shape to build against.

How it started

The first site was a Google Site. Pick a template, add content, let the service assemble the page. It worked, and it was the right tool for someone who knew nothing about how the web was put together. It also left almost no creative control, which is what eventually made it unsatisfying.

Everything after that was learned by building the thing that was needed. No course, no bootcamp, no cohort. A problem, a browser and documentation.

The first product built purely to remove a chore was RepoBundle: pulling files out of different folders in a project and combining them into one file so a model could read the whole thing. Doing that by hand once is annoying. Doing it every time is a tool waiting to exist.

Why these products exist

Each one started as an irritation, not as a market.

How the work gets done

Six habits, all of them learned from something breaking. Each one opens.

Small verified steps
What

No change lands as more than three files. Each step is tested on desktop and on a phone before the next one begins.

Why

Partial edits have broken production before. A step small enough to verify is a step that can be trusted.

In practice

This site was built that way: one three-file step at a time, with a test report between each.

Full files, never fragments
What

Any change over three lines ships as a complete file, ready to replace the old one. Smaller changes carry the exact anchor line plus one line of context above and below.

Why

A fragment pasted into the wrong place is worse than no change at all.

In practice

Every file in this repository states its repo and its exact path on the first line.

A browser is the whole toolchain
What

No terminal, no local server, no package install. Files are written in GitHub's web editor and deployed by GitHub Actions.

Why

The constraint keeps publishing honest. If putting a change live needs a local build, the change will not get published.

In practice

It is why this site has no build step and no dependencies to install, and why the same rule was applied to Weaver.

Decisions recorded with the alternative rejected
What

Every significant choice is written down next to the option it beat and the reason it won.

Why

A decision without its rejected alternative is a preference, not an argument.

In practice

Eight of them are published in the Weaver case study, from accepting text as a cut to discovering AI models at runtime.

Failures kept, not buried
What

Bugs that taught something are written up as symptom, cause, fix and lesson.

Why

The lesson is the only part that transfers to the next product.

In practice

Three are published: an empty EPUB, a plot button that failed only on phones, and a named import that blanked an entire app.

Defensive module boundaries
What

Modules import each other as namespaces and resolve functions through a candidate picker, with anything unresolved named in an in-app banner.

Why

An ES module graph fails silently and completely on one missing named export.

In practice

A blank screen became a one-line diagnostic that names the file.

Range

The constraint is chosen per product, not held as a rule.

ProductConstraint takenWhy
Weaver No framework, no bundler, no build step, zero runtime dependencies. A writing tool should open instantly and keep working offline. 16,414 lines of plain ES modules.
RepoBundle React, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS. The tree viewer, preview tabs, progress overlay and download actions are component state. A build pipeline earned its place here.
This site No framework, no bundler, no build step. It is published from a browser. A toolchain would be a barrier to publishing.
SwingMatrix Stack open. The engine runs client-side. Simulation belongs in the visitor's browser, not on a server holding anyone's scenario.

Operations, before and alongside

Most of his working life has been in operations: junior and mid-level management in retail stores and in call centres, with the longest stretch in call centres.

That is a different kind of systems work. A queue, an escalation path, a shift rota and a floor that has to keep moving are all systems with failure modes. Running them teaches things a codebase does not: what people actually do under load, which process step gets skipped when it is busy, and how one small ambiguity becomes a hundred tickets.

The software is built by someone who has been on the receiving end of bad tooling.

Working with the studio

Work is taken on where the problem is clear and the ownership is real. Three shapes fit.

Contract builds

A product taken from a stated problem to a deployed system, with the decision log and the failure log handed over alongside it.

Full-time roles

Where the job is building and owning systems end to end. The operations management background comes with it.

Collaboration

Pairing with someone who has the half that is missing, whether that is design, distribution or a domain.

Starting a conversation

Engagements are chosen for fit rather than availability. The fastest way to start is a specific problem: what exists today, what is broken about it, and what done looks like. Product questions and reader mail are welcome at the same address.

What he is building next

SwingMatrix is in development: a scenario engine for Kenyan elections combining coalition game theory with statistical simulation. It is not a political project for him. Politics is not the interest. The interest is building a model where the assumptions are exposed and the interface stays honest while the numbers move.

Queued behind it, all three tools he wants to use himself: SchemaSketch, drawing a database schema and exporting SQL with migrations. Statecraft, a visual state-machine editor that emits dependency-free JavaScript. Regex Dojo, escalating regex puzzles with hints and local progress.

There is no second hobby standing behind the work. When he is not building, he is at the day job.
